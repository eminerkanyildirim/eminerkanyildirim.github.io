About

I'm an electronic engineer who designs the PCB, writes the firmware that runs on it, and builds the software that reads and visualises its data. That means a signal-integrity decision on the board, an I2C setup in firmware, and a plotting loop in the desktop app are all part of the same job.

Day to day I work in an appliance R&D lab, verifying electronic control boards for functionality, performance and IEC 60335 safety compliance — writing the requirements, running the benchmarks, and making the calls that keep a product safe and certifiable. Alongside that, my project work sits where analog meets digital: mixed-signal boards, low-power wireless (BLE, LoRa), real-time telemetry, and the DSP that cleans up messy signals. Earlier on I helped run the electronics for a university solar-race team and interned across embedded software and automotive CAN systems.

How I work

  • Start from the real constraint — power budget, timing, cost, compliance — not the tool.
  • Prototype early, measure, and let the data settle the argument.
  • Prefer the simplest design that works: fewer parts, fewer failure modes.
  • Write firmware and tooling that the next person can actually read.

Currently exploring

Security keeps pulling at me, and it sits close to what I already do — an embedded device is only as trustworthy as the firmware inside it and the network it speaks on. I don't have a security project to show yet; what I have is a steady habit of learning:

  • Following the field closely — advisories, write-ups and the news as it breaks.
  • Daily-driving Linux, so the command line and how a system fits together are familiar ground.
  • Building up my networking fundamentals — how packets actually move, and where things break.
  • Keeping a running glossary, digging into each new security term until it clicks.

The next step is to turn that into something concrete — most likely where it overlaps my hardware and firmware work.
